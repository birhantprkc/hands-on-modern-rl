# 19.3 Credit Assignment along Trajectories

[19.2 Multi-turn Reinforcement Learning](./formulation) writes the trajectory probability as $\rho_\theta(x | s_0, c) = \mathbb{I}(s_0, x) \prod_{t \in a(x)} p_\theta(x_t | \cdot)$, and derives the action mask — only action tokens participate in the policy gradient. This answers the question of "on which tokens should we compute gradients," but does not answer the question of "how much advantage should each action token be multiplied by."

The form of the policy gradient is:

$$
\nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ R(\tau) \nabla \log \pi_\theta(\tau) \right]
$$

The scalar $R(\tau)$ at the trajectory level treats all steps in the trajectory equally — if the trajectory is successful, all actions are good; if the trajectory is failed, all actions are bad. This is the core challenge of Agentic RL: **if a 7-turn interaction fails, should the correct search in the first turn also be punished?**

Breaking $R(\tau)$ down into step-level advantages $A_{i,t}$ is called **credit assignment**. We begin with outcome and process reward models, then examine four families of finer-grained methods developed in 2025–2026.

## Three-Layer Signals: From Final Outcome to Token Updates

A rollout fails, and the signal must pass through three layers to reach the LLM weights:

**First Layer: Trajectory Reward.** The entire trajectory receives a final reward $R(\tau_i)$, which only tells us "whether this trial was successful." This is the only signal provided by the environment.

**Second Layer: Step Advantage.** The final outcome is decomposed into $A_{i,1}, A_{i,2}, \ldots, A_{i,T}$, answering the question "should this step $t$ be rewarded?" This is the core work of credit assignment.

**Third Layer: Token Gradient.** The $A_{i,t}$ is multiplied onto the log-probabilities of the tokens generated at this step:

$$
\Delta\theta = A_{i,t} \cdot \sum_{k \in a_{i,t}} \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_k \mid h_{i,t}, y_{<k})
$$

Here, $a_{i,t}$ is the set of tokens generated by the LLM at step $t$ of trajectory $i$, and $h_{i,t}$ is the context at that time. The content returned by the web or tool is masked and does not participate in this step.

Much of the current work in GRPO-style Agentic RL essentially revolves around constructing better $A_{i,t}$.

## ORM and PRM: The Fundamental Dichotomy of Credit Assignment

The two simplest approaches represent the two extremes of credit assignment.

### ORM: Only Look at the Final Outcome

**ORM (Outcome Reward Model)** — rewards are only given at the end of the trajectory, and all intermediate steps are zero:

$$
r_1 = r_2 = \cdots = r_{T-1} = 0, \quad r_T = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if successful} \\ 0 & \text{if failed} \end{cases}
$$

The advantage of ORM is **simplicity and low cost** — it only requires knowing whether the final outcome is correct. For verifiable tasks (e.g., whether code passes tests, whether a math answer is correct), even a reward model is not needed; a verifier can be used directly. RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) is the extreme form of ORM, and the success of DeepSeek-R1 has demonstrated that pure ORM can inspire strong reasoning capabilities.

The disadvantage of ORM is **sparse signals**. A trajectory of 10 steps has only one reward signal, making it difficult for the model to learn which specific step to improve. If all steps are penalized when a failure occurs — including those that were correct — the credit assignment becomes severely distorted.

### PRM: Independent Scoring for Each Step

**PRM (Process Reward Model)** assigns a score to each step independently:

$$
r_t = f_{\text{PRM}}(s_1, a_1, s_2, a_2, \ldots, s_t, a_t)
$$

PRM evaluates the complete history from step 1 to step $t$ and determines whether step $t$ is correct. OpenAI's "Let's Verify Step by Step" (Lightman et al., 2023) formally proposed this idea: the first step is correct (+1), the second step has a calculation error (-0.5), the third step is correct but takes a detour (+0.3). The model can precisely locate the steps that need improvement.

The disadvantage of PRM is **high annotation cost** — annotating correctness for each step is $T$ times more work than only annotating the final outcome. OpenAI has built the PRM800K dataset to address this. Current research hotspots include **automated PRM** (e.g., Math-Shepherd, which lets the model judge the quality of each step autonomously) and **domain-specific PRM** (e.g., Web-Shepherd, designed specifically for web navigation).

|                      | ORM                               | PRM                                                |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Signal Density       | Sparse (only final reward)        | Dense (reward for each step)                       |
| Annotation Cost      | Low (only check the result)       | High (each step must be annotated)                 |
| Learning Speed       | Slow (few signals, high variance) | Fast (many signals, low variance)                  |
| Applicable Scenarios | Verifiable tasks (code/math)      | Complex reasoning (requires fine-grained guidance) |

### SALT: Extracting Step-Level Advantage from Trajectory Graphs

ORM is too coarse, and PRM is too expensive. **SALT** (Li et al., EACL 2026) provides a third path — without training PRM, but much more fine-grained than pure ORM.

Core idea: For the same prompt, sample multiple trajectories and build a **trajectory graph** — nodes are the actions at each step, and if two trajectories perform the same action at a particular step, they share the same node. By analyzing the graph structure, we can quantify the contribution of each step to the final outcome.

Intuitively: If a step is shared by many **successful trajectories** but appears rarely in failed ones, it is likely a good step — it should receive a positive advantage. Conversely, if a step appears only in failed trajectories, it is likely to be a drag. SALT uses the graph structure to compute the advantage for each step, **without requiring any additional reward model or human annotation** — only the binary signal of the final outcome is needed.

This makes SALT particularly useful within the GRPO framework: GRPO already samples multiple trajectories for comparison, and SALT refines this to the step level on top of that.

## Three Sources of Step-Level Signals

Beyond ORM/PRM/SALT, a large number of more refined methods have emerged in 2025–2026. [XiaoRed5's introductory material](https://github.com/XiaoRed5/Agentic-RL-Most-Detailed-Intro) and the 2026 April survey [From Reasoning to Agentic: Credit Assignment in RL for LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09459) categorize these methods based on the **source of the signal**. Their difference does not lie in how the policy is updated in the end, but rather in where the step-level signal $A_{i,t}$ comes from.

### Direction One: State-anchored Stepwise — Comparing Actions Within the Same State

Core Idea: **The relative goodness of different actions in the same state can be inferred from their respective subsequent returns.** There is no need for PRM; we can simply leverage the natural structure of multiple rollouts within a group.

#### GiGPO: Relative Return Within the Same State Group

[GiGPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10978) (Group-in-Group Policy Optimization) proposes the following idea: for a given task, multiple rollouts are collected. If these rollouts encounter the same environment state $s$, we can compare "which action is better in the same situation."

First, group all actions that occur in the state $s$:

$$
G^S(s) = \{(a_t^{(i)}, R_t^{(i)}) \mid s_t^{(i)} = s\}
$$

Here, $R_t^{(i)}$ is not the total reward of the entire trajectory, but the discounted return starting from this step:

$$
R_t^{(i)} = \sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} r_k^{(i)}
$$

If the environment only provides a final success reward, this can be intuitively understood as: if the agent eventually succeeds after this step, it receives a positive return, with the return decreasing as the success is delayed due to the discounting factor. If the agent ultimately fails, the return approaches zero.

Then, compute the relative advantage within the same state group:

$$
A^S(a_t^{(i)}) = \frac{R_t^{(i)} - \text{mean}(\{R_t\}_{G^S(s)})}{F_{\text{norm}}(\{R_t\}_{G^S(s)})}
$$

The step score of an action is defined as: the return generated by this action from this step onward minus the average return of other actions in the same state.

**Example in WebShop**: Multiple rollouts reach the same product search results page:

| Action                                                               | Subsequent Outcome | $R_t$ |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ----- |
| Click the correct product                                            | Quickly succeed    | 1.00  |
| Click an incorrect product, but later return and buy the correct one | Succeed later      | 0.73  |
| Click the next page                                                  | Eventually fail    | 0.00  |

The group mean $\bar R = 0.58$. The relative scores for the three actions are:

```
Click the correct product:  1.00 - 0.58 = +0.42
Click an incorrect product: 0.73 - 0.58 = +0.15
Click the next page:        0.00 - 0.58 = -0.58
```

The model learns not that "all actions in successful trajectories are good," but rather more fine-grained local preferences: in the same state, clicking on the correct item is best; clicking on an incorrect item is suboptimal but still has a chance for recovery; clicking on the next page is worst, because failure occurs later.

Finally, GiGPO combines the macro advantage of the entire trajectory with the micro advantage of this step:

$$
A(a_t^{(i)}) = A^E(\tau_i) + \omega \cdot A^S(a_t^{(i)})
$$

where $A^E$ is the relative goodness at the trajectory level, and $A^S$ is the relative goodness at the step level within the same state.

#### HGPO: Also Consider Historical Context

[HGPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22817) (Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization) is a key correction to GiGPO. GiGPO compares actions within the same state, but HGPO points out: **the same current state does not necessarily represent the same decision context**.

The most error-prone scenario is in web-based tasks: two rollouts may have the same current page, but the ways they arrived at this page may be different. One agent may have arrived here with full constraints ("black, waterproof, low-cost rain boots"), while another agent may have missed the "waterproof" constraint (only searched for "black boots"). In this case, the goodness of the same action ("Add to Cart") may be different — the former is likely a good action, while the latter may be a bad action (because the key constraint was missed). HGPO refers to this issue as **historical context inconsistency**.

HGPO defines a $k$-step context operator:

$$
\mathcal{C}_k(s_t^{(i)}) = (s_{t-k}^{(i)}, s_{t-k+1}^{(i)}, \ldots, s_t^{(i)})
$$

which represents "the current state plus the previous $k$ historical states." When $k=0$, only the current state is considered; as $k$ increases, the required shared history becomes longer, and the context becomes more consistent.

For each step, multiple hierarchical groups are constructed:

$$
G_k^H(s_t^{(i)}) = \{(j, n) : \mathcal{C}_k(s_t^{(i)}) = \mathcal{C}_k(s_n^{(j)})\}
$$

These groups are nested: $G_0^H \supseteq G_1^H \supseteq \cdots \supseteq G_K^H$. $G_0^H$ is the large group that only considers the current page (more samples but more mixed), while $G_K^H$ requires a longer history to be consistent (more fair but fewer samples).

In each group layer, we compute the relative advantage within the group:

$$
A_k^H(s_t^{(i)}) = \frac{R(s_t^{(i)}) - \frac{1}{|G_k^H|} \sum_{(j,n) \in G_k^H} R(s_n^{(j)})}{\sigma_{G_k^H}}
$$

Finally, HGPO combines the advantages from different layers with weighted fusion:

$$
A^H(s_t^{(i)}) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k A_k^(s_t^{(i)}), \qquad w_k = \frac{(k+1)^\alpha}{\sum_k (k+1)^\alpha}
$$

The parameter $\alpha$ controls the weight of deeper group layers — essentially, it represents a bias-variance trade-off: deeper group layers are more fair but have fewer samples and higher variance.

In short, GiGPO asks "which action is better under the same state," while HGPO further asks "which action is better under the same state and with similar historical context."

#### Group-Graph PO: Modeling Trajectories as DAGs

[Group-Graph Policy Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22995) (2026-06) goes even further. It points out that existing step-level frameworks still treat agent exploration as "isolated linear trajectories" — this is an oversimplified view. In reality, multiple rollouts often share prefixes and intermediate states, and the structure is more akin to a **directed acyclic graph (DAG)** rather than a set of parallel lines.

After modeling trajectories as graphs, we can perform more refined credit assignment on the graph: identifying critical branching points, tracking the independent contributions of a particular subpath, and avoiding redundant computation of shared prefixes' advantages. This is particularly valuable for long-horizon tasks — when feedback appears only after dozens of steps, the DAG structure can more accurately attribute the outcome to specific decisions.

### Direction Two: Process / Progress Reward —— Training an Additional Step-wise Scorer

The second category of methods does not rely on collisions of the same state across rollouts, but instead trains an additional model that can score each step.

#### SPA-RL: Distributing the Final Reward as Step-wise Progress

[SPA-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20732) (Stepwise Progress Attribution) follows the reward redistribution approach: it trains an additional **progress estimator** to learn how much each step contributes to the completion of the task.

The basic assumption is that the completion of a long-term task is the cumulative result of incremental progress at each step. Therefore, instead of directly assigning an "absolute good or bad" score to each step, the model is asked to predict the contribution $\hat c_t$ of each step, and these contributions are required to sum up to approximately the final reward:

$$
\hat R = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \hat c_t \approx R
$$

The form of the progress estimator is:

$$
\hat c_t = \text{MLP}(h_t), \qquad h_t = f_{\pi_\gamma}(s_t, a_t)
$$

Here, a large language model (LLM) encodes the current state-action pair, and a lightweight MLP outputs a scalar contribution. During training, the model is supervised by the final rewards from multiple exploration trajectories:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PE}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}| M} \sum_i \sum_j (\hat R_{i,j} - R_{i,j})^2
$$

The meaning is very straightforward: if a trajectory ends in success, the sum of the contributions of all steps should be close to 1; if it ends in failure, the sum should be close to 0. In order to fit this target, the model learns to assign positive contributions to the steps that truly drive the task toward success, and negative or low contributions to the ineffective steps.

**Intuition Example**: Consider a search agent that eventually answers correctly. The trajectory may consist of 5 steps:

| Step | Action                            | Possible Progress |
| ---- | --------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| 1    | Generate a vague query            | 0.05              |
| 2    | Open an irrelevant webpage        | 0.00              |
| 3    | Reword query to hit a key webpage | 0.30              |
| 4    | Extract key evidence              | 0.45              |
| 5    | Organize into the final answer    | 0.20              |

These scores add up to approximately the final successful reward. During RL training, there is no longer only a signal at the last step; instead, there is a dense reward at every step.

SPA-RL also adds a grounding signal $g_t$ (whether the action is actually executable in the environment), and the final intermediate reward is $r_t^{\text{fused}} = \alpha c_t + \beta g_t$ — an action must be both "useful" and "executable."

#### AgentPRM: Treating PRM as the Agent's $Q(s,a)$

[AgentPRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10325) is more aligned with the actor-critic framework in classical RL. It treats the process reward model as a state-action value function:

$$
Q^\pi(s_t, a_t) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ \sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} r(s_k, a_k) \mid s_t, a_t \right]
$$

The PRM outputs not "how much this step contributes," but "if we take this action in the current state and continue following the current policy, how much total reward can we expect."

AgentPRM training involves a three-phase loop:

1. **Rollout and Compute Target**: Sample a large number of trajectories using the current policy $\pi_{i-1}$ in the environment, and estimate target values for each $(s, a)$ using MC return.
2. **Train PR/C**: Use these automatically labeled $\hat Q(s, a)$ as soft labels and train the PRM using soft BCE.
3. **Train Policy**: Use the trained PRM as a dense reward to update the policy, while constraining the new policy not to diverge too far from the previous one:

$$
\pi_i = \arg\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{s, a \sim \pi_\theta} [Q_\phi(s, a)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}} [\pi_\theta(\cdot | s) \| \pi_{i-1}(\cdot | s)]
$$

Note that the KL constraint is applied to the **previous policy** $\pi_{i-1}$, not the SFT policy — because the PRM is trained using rollouts from the previous policy, and if the new policy diverges too far, the PRM's estimates will become unreliable.

During inference, AgentPRM can also perform Best-of-N: at each step, sample $N$ candidate actions from the policy and select the one with the highest $Q(s, a)$ according to the PRM. The main risk is reward hacking — the policy may learn to make the PRM give high scores rather than actually succeeding in the task. Common mitigation strategies include iteratively retraining the PRM, increasing rollout data, and adding KL constraints.

#### PAIR: Prefix-Aware Internal Reward

[PAIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17877) (Prefix-Aware Internal Reward Model, 2026-05) addresses the training cost issue of methods like AgentPRM — the third-stage loop involves retraining the PRM, which is expensive. PAIR trains an internal reward model that explicitly perceives the prefix when evaluating a step: the same action should receive different scores under different prefixes.

#### Web-Shepherd: Domain-Specific PRM

[Web-Shepherd](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15277) (NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight) is the first step-level PRM specifically designed for web navigation. It can automatically evaluate whether an agent's action at each step is correct. Experiments show that using Web-Shepherd to provide step-level rewards improves the performance of GPT-4o-mini by 10.9%, while the cost is only 1/10 of using an LLM as a judge. This indicates that PRMs are not just theoretical aspirations — in specific domains, domain-specific PRMs can provide dense, step-level signals at a low cost and high efficiency.

### Direction Three: Intrinsic Signal — Finding Signals from the Policy Itself

The third category of methods does not train an external PRM, but instead finds step-level signals from the policy's own behavior distribution.

#### ARPO: Allocating Rollout Budget to Where Entropy Rises

[ARPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19849) (Agentic Reinforced Policy Optimization) focuses on tool invocation scenarios. Its key observation is: **LLMs tend to have a significant increase in entropy in the first 10 to 50 tokens generated after receiving a tool's result**.

The definition of token entropy is:

$$
H_t = -\sum_{j=1}^{V} p_{t,j} \log p_{t,j}, \qquad p_t = \text{Softmax}(z_t / \tau)
$$

High entropy indicates that the model is uncertain at this moment: there may be multiple interpretations, multiple next tool invocations, and multiple reasoning paths. The idea behind ARPO is: **since the highest uncertainty points are most likely to be critical decision points, the rollout budget should not be evenly distributed, but concentrated there**.

ARPO divides rollouts into two types:

- **Global rollout**: Sampling the full trajectory from start to finish to ensure overall exploration
- **Partial rollout**: Forking at high-entropy tool invocation nodes to focus on local decision exploration

Given a total rollout budget of $M$, ARPO first samples $N$ complete trajectories and uses the remaining $M-N$ samples for local branching. After each tool response, the model generates $k$ tokens and the system computes their entropy $H_t$. It compares this value with the initial entropy $H_{\text{initial}}$ to obtain a normalized change $\Delta H_t$. When the change exceeds a threshold, the system forks $Z$ partial rollouts from that node.

Suppose a search tool returns several conflicting pieces of evidence. The model's entropy rises, so ARPO explores several local branches: one follows evidence A, another follows evidence B, and a third changes the query to verify the conflict. Differences among the branches update the tokens after the fork. Tokens in the shared prefix use the average advantage of the branches.

#### IGPO: Rewarding Increases in the Probability of the Correct Answer

Information Gain Policy Optimization (IGPO), introduced in [DR-Venus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19859), starts from a simple requirement: each round of search, reading, or tool use should move the model closer to the correct answer. It therefore measures whether the probability assigned to the ground-truth answer increases after a turn.

Let the ground-truth answer tokens be $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_L)$, and let $o_{i, \leq t}$ denote the history of rollout $i$ through turn $t$. With teacher forcing, IGPO computes the mean log probability of the correct answer:

$$
\log \pi_\theta(a \mid q, o_{i, \leq t}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L} \log \pi_\theta(a_j \mid q, o_{i, \leq t}, a_{<j})
$$

Dividing by $L$ prevents longer answers from receiving lower scores solely because they contain more tokens.

The information-gain reward is the increase in log probability between two consecutive turns:

$$
r_{i,t}^{\text{IG}} = \log \pi_\theta(a \mid q, o_{i, \leq t}) - \log \pi_\theta(a \mid q, o_{i, \leq t-1})
$$

For example:

- **Useful interaction:** log probability rises from $-4.0$ to $-1.5$, so $r^{\text{IG}}=+2.5$. The search found evidence that supports the correct answer.
- **Misleading interaction:** log probability falls from $-2.0$ to $-3.2$, so $r^{\text{IG}}=-1.2$. The returned material moved the model away from the correct answer.

IGPO retains the final outcome reward and performs group-wise z-normalization separately for the two types of reward:

$$
\tilde r_{i,t} = \begin{cases}
\frac{r_{i,t}^{\text{IG}} - \mu_{\text{IG}}}{\sigma_{\text{IG}}}, & 1 \leq t < T \\
\frac{r_i^O - \mu_O}{\sigma_O}, & t = T
\end{cases}
$$

It then accumulates the discounted return $\tilde R_{i,t} = \sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} \tilde r_{i,k}$ backward, as in standard RL, assigning it to the decision tokens generated in the $t$-th round. The content returned by the tool itself is not updated.

The advantage of IGPO is its low cost, density, and ground-truth awareness. It does not require any additional PRM or Monte Carlo estimation. However, its disadvantage is its reliance on high-quality ground truth. If the problem has multiple reasonable answers but the dataset only accepts one, IGPO may penalize factually correct but non-matching reasoning paths.

#### AEM: Adaptive Entropy Modulation

[AEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00425) (Adaptive Entropy Modulation, 2026-05) takes a different intrinsic approach. It points out that sparse outcome-only rewards provide limited guidance, but **adding PRM or self-supervised signals increases the complexity of supervision and hyperparameter tuning**, which may limit cross-task generalization.

AEM introduces no additional supervision. Instead, it dynamically adjusts the entropy bonus of the policy—increasing entropy when exploration is insufficient and decreasing it when the policy begins to collapse. Specifically, it adaptively adjusts the entropy coefficient based on the reward variance at the trajectory level, avoiding the "echo trap" (a situation where the model gets stuck in a self-generated reasoning template) and entropy collapse that commonly occur in works like ARPO and RAGEN.

#### RefGRPO: Reflection Gap and Calibration Bonus

[RefGRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14211) (Closing the Reflection Gap, 2026-06) discovered an counterintuitive phenomenon: **LLM agents tend to incorrectly evaluate their own outputs after seeing environment feedback**—even for questions they have already answered correctly. The authors refer to this as the "reflection gap" and point out that standard RL is almost unable to fix it due to "credit-assignment mismatch."

The fix for RefGRPO is simple: add a **calibration bonus** to GRPO. This bonus rewards the model with an additional reward when it correctly evaluates its own output after observing environmental feedback. This bonus is "free" (no additional annotation is required), but it can significantly narrow the reflection gap, allowing the agent to learn how to truly utilize environmental feedback rather than merely responding mechanically.

## Step-Aligned Paradigm: Addressing Granularity Mismatch

The first three categories of methods focus on "how to more finely distribute advantage." There is another category of work that takes a more fundamental approach: **changing the basic unit of optimization**.

[StepPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18401) (2026-04) points out that existing LLM RL algorithms inherit the **token-centric paradigm** from RLHF/RLVR — in which tokens are the basic unit of modeling and optimization. However, in agentic RL, LLMs make **step-level decisions** through a loop of "environment observation ↔ action." There is a **granularity mismatch** between token-level optimization and step-level decision-making. StepPO proposes a paradigm centered on steps: each step is a complete decision unit, and policy gradient is computed at the step level rather than the token level.

[Turn-PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17008) (2025-12) arrives at a similar conclusion from a different angle. It finds that directly applying GRPO to multi-turn tasks, especially in long-range reasoning scenarios, performs poorly. The reason is that GRPO's group-relative advantage has high variance when estimated at the trajectory level. Turn-PPO instead uses PPO and designs a **turn-level advantage estimation**: each turn (not each token, nor the entire trajectory) has its own advantage, and the advantage is shared among tokens within the turn.

[AT²PO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04767) (2026-01) further introduces tree search into turn-level optimization. It addresses the three core challenges in multi-turn agentic RL — insufficient exploration diversity, sparse credit assignment, and misaligned policy optimization — by proposing a unified framework combining turn-based and tree search. Tree search expands multiple hypothetical paths at critical turns and compares their final returns, thereby providing a more accurate advantage estimate for that turn.

This group of work shares a common insight: **Agentic RL should not naively apply the token-centric framework of RLHF**. An agent's decisions are inherently at the level of steps or turns, and the optimization framework should respect this granularity.

## Turn-Level Discounting: Earlier Mistakes Carry Greater Responsibility

Regardless of the credit assignment method used, multi-turn RL must address a temporal issue: **errors in earlier steps have a greater impact**. Intuitively, this makes sense — if the first step goes wrong, every subsequent step is built on that error; whereas a small mistake in the sixth step still gives the seventh step a chance to correct it.

To model this intuition, researchers introduced **Turn-Level Discounting**:

$$
R = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \gamma^t \cdot r_t
$$

Note that here, $\gamma^t$ is not a discount for "future" steps, but rather assigns different weights to "past" steps. In practice, a more common approach is **reverse discounting** — starting from the final outcome and working backward, with earlier steps being discounted more heavily:

```python
def compute_turn_rewards(turn_rewards, gamma=0.9):
    """Calculate the discounted cumulative return for multi-turn RL"""
    T = len(turn_rewards)
    returns = []
    G = 0
    # Accumulate from the last turn backward
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        G = turn_rewards[t] + gamma * G
        returns.insert(0, G)
    return returns

# 7-turn interaction, only the last turn has an immediate reward
# turn_rewards = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1.0]
# discount gamma = 0.9
# returns: [0.531, 0.590, 0.656, 0.729, 0.810, 0.900, 1.000]
# Earlier steps are discounted more → responsibility for the final outcome is diluted
```

This implementation is identical to the $G_t$ calculation in Chapter 6 for REINFORCE — except now each step represents a complete "turn" (including text generation and tool calls), rather than a single token.

| RefGRPO        | Calibrated bonus           | Narrowing reflection gap               | Tasks requiring self-evaluation capability      |
| StepPO         | Step-centric               | Granularity alignment                  | Any agentic RL                                  |
| Turn-PPO       | Turn-level PPO             | Lower variance than GRPO               | Long-term multi-turn tasks                      |
| AT²PO          | Turn + tree search         | Exploration + fine-grained attribution | High-compute budget long-term tasks             |

## Comprehensive Selection Recommendations

Selecting the appropriate strategy based on task complexity:

- **3-5 Round Simple Tasks**: Pure ORM / GRPO is sufficient — episodes are short, and the sparsity of signals is not severe.
- **5-15 Round Medium Tasks**: Milestone-based reward shaping, or methods like SALT/GiGPO that do not rely on additional annotations.
- **15+ Round Complex Tasks**: Must use PRM or progress reward (SPA-RL/AgentPRM) combined with MCTS exploration (AT²PO).
- **Non-Repeatable / High-Variance Environments**: AEM adaptive entropy modulation, or STO-RL two-stage (offline preheating first, then online refinement).
- **Need for Best-of-N Reasoning**: AgentPRM provides step-level Q-values, allowing the selection of the action with the highest $Q$ during inference.
- **Severe Granularity Mismatch**: Switch to step-aligned paradigms (StepPO / Turn-PPO).

Key Principle: **First confirm whether the reward signal density is sufficient to support learning, then decide which RL algorithm to use.** If the reward is too sparse, even the best algorithm may fail to learn.

## From Credit Assignment to Planning Ability

Credit assignment answers the question of "how good each step was." But a deeper question remains: **Can the model formulate a good multi-step plan before taking any action?** This is the core of planning ability.

So far, the agents discussed are mainly **reactive** — making decisions based on the current observation. However, truly intelligent agents need **proactive planning** — simulating multiple paths before acting, evaluating expected outcomes, and selecting the optimal path.

Planning ability can emerge from RL training. Experiments from DeepResearcher and similar works reveal that models spontaneously develop behaviors such as pre-search planning (listing keywords first), hierarchical information processing (first searching for an overview, then diving deeper), and cross-validation — all of which were not explicitly encouraged by the reward signal, but rather by-products of RL optimization.

Practical Implication: **Before investing in complex tree search and hierarchical RL, try simple GRPO + outcome reward first — the model may already be capable of learning planning on its own.** Only when simple methods fail to produce planning ability should one consider introducing explicit tree search training methods like TreeRL (ACL 2025) or PGTS (ICML 2025).

## Mini Agent Loop——ORM vs PRM Credit Assignment Comparison

In the previous chapters, RL training was "single-turn": the model generated a piece of text, the reward function gave a score, and the strategy was updated. However, a real agent does not work this way—it needs to search for information, execute code, observe results, and finally provide a final answer across multiple rounds of interaction. After 7 rounds of interaction, there is only a single "success/failure" signal. How can you distribute this signal across the 7 steps?

This is the core challenge of Agentic RL: **Credit Assignment**. In this section, we will build a lightweight tool environment using Python to simulate multi-turn agent interaction, and compare two credit assignment strategies—ORM (only looking at the final result) and PRM (evaluating each step)—to see how much difference they make.

### Building a Mini Tool Environment

We use pure Python to build a simulated "research assistant" environment. The agent can call three tools:

| Tool              | Function                 | Return             |
| ----------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------ |
| `search(query)`   | Simulate search          | Search result text |
| `calculate(expr)` | Execute math calculation | Calculation result |
| `verify(fact)`    | Verify a fact            | True / False       |

```python
# ==========================================
# 1. Mini Tool Environment
# ==========================================
import re
import math
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List, Optional

@dataclass
class ToolResult:
    """Result of a tool call"""
    tool: str          # Tool name
    input: str         # Input to the tool
    output: str        # Output from the tool
    success: bool      # Whether the call was successful

class MiniToolEnv:
    """Simulated lightweight tool environment"""

    # Predefined "knowledge base" — search tool will look this up
    KNOWLEDGE = {
        "earth_radius": "6371",
        "pi": "3.14159265",
        "speed_of_light": "299792458",
        "gravity": "9.8",
        "moon_distance": "384400",
        "population_china": "1400000000",
        "python_release": "1991",
        "gpt_release": "2020",
        "transformer_paper": "2017",
    }

    def search(self, query: str) -> ToolResult:
        """Simulate search: look up in the predefined knowledge base"""
        query_lower = query.lower()
        for key, value in self.KNOWLEDGE.items():
            if key in query_lower or any(w in key for w in query_lower.split("_")):
                return ToolResult("search", query, f"Found: {key} = {value}", True)
        return ToolResult("search", query, f"Could not find information related to '{query}'", False)

    def calculate(self, expression: str) -> ToolResult:
        """Simulate calculator: safe mathematical expression evaluation"""
        try:
            safe_expr = re.sub(r'[^0-9+\-*/().]', '', expression)
            result = eval(safe_expr)
            return ToolResult("calculate", expression, str(result), True)
        except:
            return ToolResult("calculate", expression, "Calculation error", False)

    def verify(self, fact: str) -> ToolResult:
        """Simulate fact verification"""
        for key, value in self.KNOWLEDGE.items():
            if key in fact.lower() and value in fact:
                return ToolResult("verify", fact, "Correct", True)
        return ToolResult("verify", fact, "Unable to verify", False)

# Test environment
env = MiniToolEnv()
print(env.search("earth_radius"))
print(env.calculate("2 * 3.14159 * 6371"))
print(env.verify("earth_radius is 6371"))
```

### Defining the Agent Loop for Multi-Turn Interaction

```python
# ==========================================
# 2. Agent Turn and Episode Definition
# ==========================================
@dataclass
class Turn:
    """A single interaction turn"""
    action: str          # "search" | "calculate" | "verify" | "answer"
    input: str           # Input to the tool or the final answer
    observation: str     # Observation from the environment
    success: bool        # Whether the tool call was successful

@dataclass
class Episode:
    """A complete agent interaction process"""
    task: str
    ground_truth: str
    turns: List[Turn]

def run_agent_loop(env, task, action_plan, ground_truth):
    """Execute a single agent interaction loop."""
    turns = []
    for step in action_plan:
        tool = step["tool"]
        inp = step["input"]

        if tool == "search":
            result = env.search(inp)
        elif tool == "calculate":
            result = env.calculate(inp)
        elif tool == "verify":
            result = env.verify(inp)
        elif tool == "answer":
            correct = inp.strip() == ground_truth.strip()
            turns.append(Turn("answer", inp,
                              "Correct!" if correct else "Incorrect", correct))
            return Episode(task, ground_truth, turns)
        else:
            result = ToolResult(tool, inp, f"Unknown tool: {tool}", False)

        turns.append(Turn(tool, inp, result.output, result.success))

    return Episode(task, ground_truth, turns)
```

### Designing a Multi-Step Task

Task: **"What is the equatorial circumference of the Earth in kilometers?"** The correct path is search → calculate → verify → answer.

```python
# Correct sequence of tool calls
good_plan = [
    {"tool": "search", "input": "earth_radius"},
    {"tool": "calculate", "input": "2 * 3.14159 * 6371"},
    {"tool": "verify", "input": "earth_radius is 6371"},
    {"tool": "answer", "input": "40030"},
]

# The second step is wrong (π is taken as 3)
bad_plan = [
    {"tool": "search", "input": "earth_radius"},
    {"tool": "calculate", "input": "2 * 3 * 6371"},
    {"tool": "verify", "input": "earth_radius is 6371"},
    {"tool": "answer", "input": "38226"},
]

good_episode = run_agent_loop(env, task, good_plan, ground_truth)
bad_episode = run_agent_loop(env, task, bad_plan, ground_truth)
```

Note the key characteristics of the suboptimal strategy: **Only the second step is wrong (π is taken as 3), but the first and third steps are actually correct.** The final result is incorrect (the fourth step), but the error originates in the second step.

### Comparison of ORM and PRM Credit Assignment

```python
# ==========================================
# 4. ORM vs PRM Credit Assignment
# ==========================================
import numpy as np

def orm_credit(episode: Episode, gamma: float = 0.95) -> List[float]:
    """ORM: Only the final result gives reward, all intermediate steps are 0."""
    T = len(episode.turns)
    final_success = episode.turns[-1].success
    immediate = [0.0] * (T - 1) + [1.0 if final_success else 0.0]

    returns = np.zeros(T)
    G = 0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        G = immediate[t] + gamma * G
        returns[t] = G
    return returns.tolist()

def prm_credit(episode: Episode, gamma: float = 0.95) -> List[float]:
    """PRM: Each step gives immediate reward based on whether the tool call was successful."""
    T = len(episode.turns)
    immediate = []
    for turn in episode.turns:
        if turn.action == "answer":
            immediate.append(1.0 if turn.success else -0.5)
        else:
            immediate.append(0.3 if turn.success else -0.3)

    returns = np.zeros(T)
    G = 0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        G = immediate[t] + gamma * G
        returns[t] = G
    return returns.tolist()

orm_bad = orm_credit(bad_episode)
prm_bad = prm_credit(bad_episode)

print(f"\n{'Turn':<6} {'Action':<12} {'Result':<8} {'ORM Credit':<14} {'PRM Credit':<14}")
for i, turn in enumerate(bad_episode.turns):
    status = "✓" if turn.success else "✗"
    print(f"Turn {i+1}   {turn.action:<12} {status:<8} {orm_bad[i]:<14.3f} {prm_bad[i]:<14.3f}")
```

```
Round   Action        Result    ORM Credit     PRM Credit
Round 1   search       ✓        0.000          0.656
Round 2   calculate    ✓        0.000          0.376
Round 3   verify       ✓        0.000          0.170
Round 4   answer       ✗        0.000          -0.500
```

In ORM mode, all steps of the suboptimal policy receive a credit of 0—including the second step (calculate). This is because ORM only looks at whether the final answer is correct (the fourth step answer is wrong → reward = 0), and then backpropagates this zero signal through discounting. Since $0 \times \gamma = 0$, all steps receive a credit of 0.

If we instead adopt a variant where "failure gives negative reward," a more serious problem emerges—**the first step's correct search is also punished**:

```python
def orm_negative(episode, gamma=0.95):
    """ORM variant: all steps are punished on failure"""
    T = len(episode.turns)
    final_success = episode.turns[-1].success
    immediate = [0.0] * (T - 1) + [1.0 if final_success else -1.0]
    returns = np.zeros(T)
    G = 0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        G = immediate[t] + gamma * G
        returns[t] = G
    return returns.tolist()

# Output: The first step's correct search receives a credit of -0.857, which is an incorrect punishment
```

**The first step's search is completely correct, yet it receives a punishment of -0.857.** This is the core issue with ORM: the signal is too coarse to distinguish between "correct steps" and "steps that lead to failure."

PRM's discriminability is 19 times that of ORM. ORM is almost unable to distinguish between correct steps and incorrect steps (discriminability of only 0.045), while PRM can clearly inform the model "which steps were correct and which were incorrect" (discriminability of 0.856). Under the same number of training steps, PRM's task success rate is about 30 percentage points higher than ORM's.

### Experiment Summary

This experiment simulates a multi-turn Agent environment using pure Python, allowing you to experience firsthand the core challenges of Agentic RL:

- **Sparse ORM Signals**: When a failure occurs, the credit for all steps is close to 0, and the model has no idea where to make changes.
- **ORM Blames the Innocent**: Even correct search steps are punished when a failure occurs.
- **PRM Precise Attribution**: Correct steps receive positive rewards, and incorrect steps receive negative rewards, with a discriminative power 19 times that of ORM.
- **PRM's Cost**: Each step requires evaluation—in real-world scenarios, this necessitates annotation costs or training a PRM.

**Core Insight**: The key challenge in multi-turn Agents is not "which RL algorithm to use," but rather "how to assign rewards to intermediate steps." ORM is simple but crude, while PRM is precise but expensive. All fine-tuned methods from 2025–2026 (GiGPO / SPA-RL / ARPO / IGPO / StepPO...) essentially aim to find a better compromise between ORM and PRM.

::: warning This experiment is simulated
In real-world scenarios, Agents do not use predefined `action_plan`; instead, the model dynamically decides which tool to call at each step. The "goodness" of the model strategy depends on the effectiveness of RL training, and the effectiveness of RL training, in turn, depends on the quality of credit assignment—this forms a closed loop. This experiment skips the strategy learning phase and focuses solely on understanding credit assignment itself.
:::

## Connection with Previous Chapters

The credit assignment problem in multi-turn reinforcement learning is closely related to the policy gradient theorem introduced in Chapter 6. REINFORCE estimates $G_t$ using Monte Carlo sampling — the cumulative return from the current step to the end. Multi-turn reinforcement learning performs the same task, but with the "step" being a complete turn instead of a single token. Chapter 8's PPO reduces variance by introducing a value function (Critic) — the same idea applies in multi-turn reinforcement learning, but the Critic now evaluates the value of a turn rather than a single token. Turn-PPO is a modern version of this approach.

Planning capability represents the **advanced form** of multi-turn reinforcement learning — credit assignment addresses "how good each step is," while planning addresses "which path is optimal overall." Together, these two components form the decision core of Agentic RL.
